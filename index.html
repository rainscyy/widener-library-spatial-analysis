Before the process

What we observed

Our question across both assignments: how do two people’s readings of concrete elements and abstract spatial qualities, taught through the images each of us chose, turn into different ways for a model to read the same walk through Widener Library — and what does a model find when we stop naming the categories?

A3 · observation

Same verdict on most frames, but not the same way.

Raine’s and Chen’s models pick the same class on 90.4% of frames for arch/door and 87.4% for open/enclosed. Yet the scores correlate more for open/enclosed (0.83) than for arch/door (0.72). Agreement between models is not accuracy against the space.

A3 · observation

“Arch” wins almost everywhere.

Both arch/door models call most frames arch (Raine 96%, Chen 88%). Each model must choose one of two words; it cannot say “both” or “neither”.

A4 · observation

Without labels, the maps follow the walk.

89–96% of consecutive frames share a cluster (17–21% if shuffled). Long strands on every map are continuous stretches of the route.

A4 · observation → reading

Depth groups space by form, across materials.

The depth autoencoder puts brick and marble corridors together and separates close flat surfaces. These two clusters also have the lowest A3 “open” scores (0.34, 0.32) — a link we read as suggestive, not proven.

Assignment 3 · group + individual

Classifiers: the environment as a signal

The brief, and where each part is

  1. Record a walkthrough of a space → a 3:02 vertical walk through Widener Library (video above).
  2. Split it into ≥1000 frames → 1000 evenly spaced frames (frame overview).
  3. Two category sets, one concrete, one abstract → arch / door and open / enclosed (why).
  4. ≥20 images per category per student → Raine 96, Chen 80 (image sets).
  5. One classifier per set per student → 4 models (method).
  6. Top 5 frames per category and category strength over time → top 5 · interactive timelines.
  7. Conclusions on how our choices shaped the models → results · limits.
01

Why arch / door and open / enclosed intent

We chose arch / door and open / enclosed to compare concrete architectural elements with an abstract spatial experience. An arch frames an opening you can pass through; a door changes access and visibility with a solid, movable surface that can be open, half-open or closed. Both shape how we move and see, but neither decides whether a space feels open. Open / enclosed is about experience, not inside / outside or door open / closed: light, scale, long sightlines and continuous movement can all play a part, so an interior can feel open and an outdoor space enclosed.

We chose images independently, expecting more agreement on concrete elements and more room for interpretation on abstract qualities, and applied all four models to the same walk, so we could see when they agree, when they diverge, and go back to those exact frames. This was our expectation going in, not a result.

03

Method: our image rules, one shared recipe

Image rulesarch: an arched outline, an opening, a passage · door: a physical door leaf, open, half-open or closed · arched doors fit both, so each of us decided how to file them
ImagesRaine: open 25 · enclosed 24 · arch 23 · door 24 · Chen: 20 per class
ModelCLIP ViT-B/32 (datacomp_xl_s13b_b90k) backbone, frozen · only a linear head is trained
Training50 epochs · learning rate 0.001 · seed 42 · no random-crop augmentation, to keep composition
Splitabout 80 / 20 per class · held-out: Raine 10 images per model (5 per class), Chen 8
Outputtwo-class scores for each of the 1000 frames · top 5 per class · timelines synced to the video

Only the classification head is trained, not CLIP itself. Each model is forced to choose between its two classes; there is no “both” or “neither”.

06

Limits

  • Tiny held-out sets. All four models reach 100% held-out accuracy, on 8–10 images each. That says the training classes are separable, not that the models are right on the walk.
  • Shortcuts. Our arch and door images may also differ in colour (our arches lean grey, doors wooden), material, framing and source; our open images lean bright and large, enclosed darker and narrow. A model may learn brightness or colour instead of the concept.
  • Duplicates. Raine’s concrete set contains one exact duplicate pair (both in training); near-duplicates or same-scene leakage were not fully ruled out.
  • Crop. CLIP resizes and centre-crops to a square, so the top and bottom of our vertical frames do not reach the model.
  • Scores are relative. A two-class score is not an objective measure of openness, and a higher score does not mean a more accurate model.

Learning to read space.

Elements & space

We chose arch / door and open / enclosed to connect architectural elements with the experience of space. The arches we focused on are fixed, often grey, and frame passage through an opening. Doors introduce a solid, movable surface within a smaller threshold: they can be open, half-open, or closed. Both shape access and visibility, but neither determines whether a space feels open or enclosed.

Independent readings

We expect our understanding of concrete elements to be more consistent, leading us to select similar images of arches and doors. Abstract qualities allow more interpretation: one person may read openness through daylight, another through long sightlines or freedom of movement.

A common walkthrough

By applying our independently trained models to the same video, we can test this expectation. Similar concrete-classification curves would suggest shared recognition. Differences in the abstract curves—especially at the same moments—may reveal how our image choices taught the models different readings of space. We will examine those frames to understand where the readings agree and where they diverge.

Assignment 4 · Part 1

Autoencoders: categories we did not name

In A3 we set the categories and the models chose between them. Here we train autoencoders on the same frames in three versions — RGB, depth and edges — and ask: without being told arch, door, open or enclosed, how does a model organise the same library? Do changes in colour, texture, outline and depth bring its groups closer to our categories or further away? People still set the data, preprocessing, network, training, normalisation, projection and clustering, so these groups are one possible ordering, not an objective truth about the space.

The brief, and where each part is

  1. Take the classifier video frames and make two more datasets, depth and edges → datasets.
  2. Train one autoencoder per dataset (3 in total) → training.
  3. Map emergent categories with the embedding map and its clustering tools → interactive map above and maps.
  4. Experiment with and record projection × clustering combinations → combinations.
  5. Reflect on the categories and show archetypes per category per dataset → archetypes and autoencoder vs classifier.
1000 video frames→RGB · Depth Anything V2 · Canny edges→3 CNN autoencoders

02

Same autoencoder, three inputs

An autoencoder learns to squeeze each image into a short code (here 128 numbers) and rebuild it. Nobody tells it what to look for, so frames with similar codes are frames the model finds similar. We kept every setting identical so differences come from the input alone, and ran a 5-epoch test first to check speed and reconstructions.

ModelCNN autoencoder · 64×64 input · latent 128 · 2.14 M parameters
Training50 epochs · lr 0.001 · batch 64 · 10% held out · augmentation: horizontal flip + random crop (min 0.6)
Final loss (train / held-out)RGB 0.0068 / 0.0084 · Depth 0.0015 / 0.0015 · Edges 0.0027 / 0.0035
HardwareMacBook Air (Apple MPS) · about 8, 5 and 3 minutes

Losses are not comparable across datasets: depth maps are smooth gradients and edge maps are mostly black, so both are easy to rebuild. Held-out frames sit next to training frames in time, so held-out loss says little about generalisation. Thin edges almost vanish at 64 px, and the edges model reconstructs least.

Semantic topography

Brief: use the self-organising maps from the embeddings to design a tile wall relief or landscape, with grey as height. Consider the number of tiles, how tight they sit, and whether clusters map to materials or colours.

01

Our mapping

Dataset = the Widener depth frames, so Part 1 and Part 2 read the same space. Each tile is one frame; tiles are laid out by the depth autoencoder’s map (rows by map y, each row by map x) so neighbours stay neighbours; tile height = the frame’s depth grey (near = high); colour or shape = its depth cluster. Notes & limits

  • Time dominates. Frames 0.18 s apart are near-duplicates; clusters partly encode “where in the walk”, not only what a frame looks like.
  • Square crops. Classifiers and autoencoders both centre-crop the vertical frames, so floors and ceilings are partly cut.
  • One parameter set. Archetypes and readings use UMAP (15 neighbours) + k-means k = 8. Other k values would split or merge groups.
  • Parameters were set by rule, not by look: same autoencoder settings for all three datasets; DBSCAN eps from a fixed percentile.
  • Tools. Teacher’s autoencoder app v2 (Flask + PyTorch), patched only to run on Apple MPS and to use relative folders. Analysis numbers come from the app’s own vectors and clusters.
